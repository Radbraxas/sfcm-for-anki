# Light-dependent reactions of photosynthesis

The light-dependent reactions take place in the thylakoid membranes of the chloroplast. Their job is to convert light energy into chemical energy in two carrier molecules, ATP and NADPH, which the Calvin cycle later uses to fix carbon dioxide into sugar.

Light is absorbed by pigments, mainly chlorophyll a and chlorophyll b, arranged in two large complexes called photosystem II and photosystem I. Chlorophyll absorbs red and blue light strongly and reflects green light, which is why leaves look green.

The sequence begins at photosystem II. Absorbed light excites an electron in the reaction-center chlorophyll, called P680, and the electron is passed to a primary acceptor. To replace it, photosystem II splits water molecules. This step, called photolysis, releases oxygen gas, protons and electrons. All of the oxygen released by plants comes from water, not from carbon dioxide.

The excited electrons travel down an electron transport chain that includes plastoquinone, the cytochrome b6f complex and plastocyanin. As they move, energy is used to pump protons from the stroma into the thylakoid space, building a proton gradient.

At photosystem I, light excites the electrons again, at the reaction center P700. They are then passed to ferredoxin and finally to the enzyme NADP+ reductase, which uses them to reduce NADP+ to NADPH.

Protons flow back out of the thylakoid space through ATP synthase, and this flow drives the synthesis of ATP from ADP and phosphate. This mechanism is called chemiosmosis, and the overall process is called photophosphorylation.
